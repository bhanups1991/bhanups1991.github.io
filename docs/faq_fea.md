## 1. Weak Forms and Boundary Conditions

**Q: How does strong-form equilibrium become the FE weak form?**
Starting from pointwise balance $-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = \mathbf b$, multiply by an admissible virtual displacement $\delta\mathbf u$ and integrate by parts:

$$\int_\Omega \boldsymbol\sigma:\nabla\delta\mathbf u\,dV = \int_\Omega \mathbf b\cdot\delta\mathbf u\,dV + \int_{\Gamma_t}\mathbf t\cdot\delta\mathbf u\,dS$$

Integration by parts always produces a boundary flux term; what happens to it depends on how the boundary conditions are treated. This is the same derivation for the scalar Poisson case: $-\nabla^2 u = f \Rightarrow \int_\Omega \nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx - \int_{\partial\Omega}(\nabla u\cdot n)\,v\,ds = \int_\Omega fv\,dx$.

**Q: What's the difference between essential and natural boundary conditions?**
**Essential** BCs prescribe the primary variable (displacement) and define the admissible function space itself. **Natural** BCs prescribe a derived quantity (traction) and fall out automatically from the boundary term generated by integration by parts. This is why a traction-free edge needs no explicit code — the weak form already "knows" $\mathbf t = 0$ there.

**Q: Why does the boundary integral vanish in standard (strong) Dirichlet enforcement?**
Because test functions are chosen from $H^1_0(\Omega)$ — the subspace of $H^1$ functions with zero trace (zero boundary values) by construction. Since $v=0$ on $\partial\Omega$, $\int_{\partial\Omega}(\nabla u\cdot n)v\,ds \equiv 0$ automatically; it isn't dropped or approximated, the space definition kills it. The trial function then lives in the affine space $u \in u_D + H^1_0(\Omega)$: search over all $H^1_0$ corrections on top of a fixed boundary lift $u_D$. This is exactly what applying a `DirichletBC` does numerically — pin the boundary DOFs, solve for the interior ones.

**Q: What is Nitsche's method and why does it exist?**
Nitsche's method enforces Dirichlet BCs **weakly**, through extra terms in the variational form, rather than by constraining the function space. It's needed whenever strong enforcement is inconvenient — unfitted meshes, embedded interfaces, or contact, where the constraint boundary doesn't align with the mesh. Both $u$ and $v$ now live in the *full* $H^1(\Omega)$, so the boundary integral no longer vanishes and must be dealt with explicitly.

**Q: What are the two extra terms Nitsche adds, and what does each do?**
Starting from the integrated-by-parts form with a non-vanishing boundary integral, two corrections are added:

$$-\int_{\partial\Omega}(\nabla v\cdot n)(u-u_D)\,ds \;+\; \frac{\alpha}{h}\int_{\partial\Omega}(u-u_D)\,v\,ds$$

- **Symmetry term** $-\int_{\partial\Omega}(\nabla v\cdot n)(u-u_D)\,ds$: the transpose of the flux term already present, restoring $a(u,v)=a(v,u)$. Without it the system matrix is non-symmetric, breaking standard solvers and error analysis.
- **Penalty/coercivity term** $\frac{\alpha}{h}\int_{\partial\Omega}(u-u_D)v\,ds$: enforces $u\approx u_D$. $\alpha$ must be large enough (above a mesh/trace-inequality-dependent threshold) for coercivity; $h$ (element diameter) ensures the penalty scales correctly under mesh refinement.

**Q: Why are both Nitsche terms needed — can one be dropped?**
Neither alone suffices: penalty-only is coercive but not symmetric (suboptimal convergence order); symmetry-only is symmetric but not coercive (the system may be singular). Together they give a form that is **consistent** (the exact solution satisfies it), **symmetric**, and **coercive** — the three properties needed for optimal convergence.

**Q: What is the final Nitsche bilinear/linear form?**

$$a(u,v) = \int_\Omega \nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx + \int_{\partial\Omega}\Big[-(n\cdot\nabla u)v - (n\cdot\nabla v)u + \tfrac{\alpha}{h}uv\Big]ds$$
$$L(v) = \int_\Omega fv\,dx + \int_{\partial\Omega}\Big[-(n\cdot\nabla v)u_D + \tfrac{\alpha}{h}u_D v\Big]ds$$

All $u$-dependent terms go into $a(u,v)$; known Dirichlet data $u_D$ becomes right-hand-side data in $L(v)$ — the standard split producing $Au=b$. The same skeleton, with a one-sided inequality instead of an equality constraint, underlies Nitsche-based contact enforcement (see the Contact Mechanics FAQ).

## 2. Isoparametric Elements and the Jacobian

**Q: What's the one-sentence idea behind isoparametric elements?**
Use the **same shape functions** for the geometry map (reference → physical element) and for interpolating the solution. "Iso" = same, "parametric" = the coordinate parametrization; one set of shape functions does both jobs.

**Q: What problem do isoparametric elements actually solve?**
Two, simultaneously: (1) straight-edged elements on curved domains leave a permanent geometry error that never vanishes no matter how the element degree is raised; curved isoparametric elements hug the real geometry. (2) Gauss quadrature and shape-function differentiation are only clean on a standard reference shape (bi-unit square, unit triangle) — isoparametric elements let all calculus happen once on that reference element $\hat K$, then map results back.

**Q: How does the geometry map work, and how is the solution interpolated the same way?**
For element $K$ with $n$ nodes at $\{\mathbf x_i\}$:

$$\mathbf x(\boldsymbol\xi) = \sum_{i=1}^n N_i(\boldsymbol\xi)\,\mathbf x_i, \qquad u_h(\boldsymbol\xi) = \sum_{i=1}^n N_i(\boldsymbol\xi)\,u_i$$

Same $N_i$, same $\boldsymbol\xi$ — physical coordinates interpolated from nodal coordinates, solution interpolated from nodal solution values. Note: $\boldsymbol\xi$ are purely computational (parent) coordinates and are *not* automatically the physical reference configuration of continuum mechanics (see next question, and the Continuum Mechanics FAQ).

**Q: What is the Jacobian, and what are its two critical uses?**

$$J = \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(\xi,\eta)}, \qquad \frac{\partial x}{\partial \xi} = \sum_i \frac{\partial N_i}{\partial\xi}x_i$$

1. **Physical gradients of shape functions** (needed for $\nabla u_h$ in the weak form): $\big[\partial N_i/\partial x,\ \partial N_i/\partial y\big]^T = J^{-1}\big[\partial N_i/\partial\xi,\ \partial N_i/\partial\eta\big]^T$.
2. **Change of variables for integration**: $\int_K f\,dx\,dy = \int_{\hat K} f\,|\det J|\,d\xi\,d\eta$.

$|\det J|$ is the local area/volume scaling factor. If $\det J = 0$ anywhere inside the element, the map is degenerate and the stiffness matrix is garbage — the deeper reason mesh quality matters. A negative $\det J$ signals an inverted element under the standard mapping convention.

**Q: Is Gauss quadrature exact for isoparametric elements?**
Generally no. Gauss quadrature is exact only for polynomials up to a specified degree, but the isoparametric transformation introduces $J^{-1}$ and $\det J$ into the integrand, making it rational rather than polynomial for any element whose geometry map isn't affine (i.e. anything but a parallelepiped/straight-sided simplex). So for general quadrilaterals, hexahedra, or curved elements, finite-point quadrature is a numerical **approximation**, with accuracy depending on element order, distortion, and the chosen integration rule.

**Q: What are the sub-, iso-, and superparametric variants?**

| Variant | Geometry order | Solution order | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subparametric | Lower | Higher | Flat-sided elements with high-order solution; rare |
| Isoparametric | Same | Same | Standard workhorse of FEM |
| Superparametric | Higher | Lower | Exact geometry, coarse solution; specialized |

**Q: Is the isoparametric map valid for any element with any number of nodes?**
Yes, with one critical condition: $F_K:\hat K\to K$ must be a **bijection** — one-to-one, onto, with $\det J \neq 0$ everywhere inside the element. This holds for 2-node lines, linear quads/triangles, quadratic serendipity/curved elements, 20-node hexahedra, and in principle any $n$. The danger is **badly placed midside nodes**: moving one too far toward a corner can drive $\det J$ to zero or negative somewhere inside the element, meaning the map folds over itself. Mesh-quality checks exist precisely to guarantee the isoparametric map stays a valid bijection everywhere.

**Q: How does the isoparametric reference element relate to the reference/current configuration of continuum mechanics? Are they the same thing?**
No — same language, different objects, and this trips up almost everyone:

| | Continuum mechanics | Isoparametric FEM |
|---|---|---|
| Domain 1 | Undeformed body $\mathcal B_0$, coords $\mathbf X$ | Reference element $\hat K$, coords $\boldsymbol\xi$ |
| Domain 2 | Deformed body $\mathcal B_t$, coords $\mathbf x$ | Physical element $K$, coords $\mathbf x$ |
| Map | Deformation $\boldsymbol\varphi(\mathbf X)$ — **physics** | Element map $F_K(\boldsymbol\xi)$ — **bookkeeping** |
| Jacobian | $\mathbf F = \nabla_X\boldsymbol\varphi$ — carries stress/strain | $J = \partial\mathbf x/\partial\boldsymbol\xi$ — carries integration weights, gradients |
| Meaning | Material is actually moving | Nothing moves; pure change of variables |

The $\hat K \to K$ isoparametric map is purely mathematical; the $\mathcal B_0 \to \mathcal B_t$ continuum map is physical. This also explains why a mesh Jacobian remains necessary even for an Eulerian FE formulation whose spatial mesh does *not* follow material particles: the discretization still needs a parent-to-physical mapping, entirely separate from the material deformation gradient $\mathbf F$.

**Q: How do the two Jacobians interact in geometrically nonlinear problems?**
Three configurations coexist: $\hat K \xrightarrow{J_0} K_0 \xrightarrow{\mathbf F} K_t$, where $\hat K\to K_0$ is the isoparametric map to the (fixed) undeformed mesh and $K_0\to K_t$ is the physical deformation. The total Jacobian is $J_t = \mathbf F\,J_0$. In a **Total Lagrangian** formulation, integration stays over the reference configuration using $J_0$ — the mesh coordinates never change — and all the physics of deformation is carried by $\mathbf F$ inside the constitutive/kinematic expressions.

**Q: What happens if isoparametric elements aren't used?**
Two options, both bad:
- **Subparametric** (straight-edged geometry, higher-order solution): geometry error persists no matter the solution polynomial order — a curved boundary stays a polygon forever; only $h$-refinement reduces it.
- **Shape functions defined directly in physical space**: theoretically possible, practically disastrous — every element has a different shape, so nothing (Gauss points, shape-function tables) can be reused, and constructing well-behaved polynomials on distorted/curved physical elements is genuinely hard. The efficiency of assembly collapses.

Isoparametric elements aren't mathematically mandatory; they're the dominant choice because they make implementation uniform, efficient, and mathematically clean across all element shapes.

**Q: Why do higher-order elements require matching geometry order to solution order?**
Two error sources exist simultaneously: geometry approximation of $\partial\Omega$ and solution approximation of $u$. The classical estimate is

$$\|u-u_h\|_{H^1} \le C\big(h^k|u|_{H^{k+1}} + h^{p+1}|\text{geometry error}|\big)$$

where $k$ is the solution polynomial degree and $p$ the geometry-map degree. If $k>p$, the geometry error dominates and the extra solution accuracy is wasted. Isoparametric elements enforce $k=p$ so neither error pollutes the other; in DOLFINx-style code, `("Lagrange", 2)` upgrades both consistently. Linear elements give $O(h^1)$ in the $H^1$ norm with flat edges, quadratic $O(h^2)$ with curved edges, etc. — going up an order changes the **convergence rate**, not just a constant factor.

**Q: Is there a subtlety with higher-order elements under geometric nonlinearity?**
Yes. The isoparametric map describes fixed mesh geometry; the deformation gradient $\mathbf F$ is computed from the displacement field $u_h$, whose polynomial order is **one less** than $u_h$ itself (differentiation drops the degree by one). So with quadratic displacement elements, strains — and hence stresses — are only linear within each element. A high-order quadrature rule tuned to expect higher-order stress variation will not find it; this is one reason element-order choices in nonlinear solid mechanics need more care than in linear problems.

## 3. Locking, Hourglassing, and Cures

**Q: Shear locking vs. volumetric locking — how do they compare?**

| | Shear locking | Volumetric locking |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | bending, linear elements | near-incompressibility (any order, worst for linear) |
| Constraint over-enforced | transverse shear strain $\approx 0$ (Kirchhoff limit) | $\operatorname{div}(\mathbf u)=0$ pointwise |
| Material-dependent? | No — geometry/interpolation issue | Yes — tied to Poisson's ratio/material law |
| Typical fix | reduced integration, quadratic elements, incompatible modes | mixed u–p formulation, B-bar, selective reduced integration |

Both are the same underlying phenomenon: the discrete displacement space cannot represent the physical zero-strain constraint (zero shear in pure bending; zero volumetric strain at incompressibility) without picking up spurious strain energy, which artificially stiffens the element.

**Q: What's the mechanism of shear locking for a low-order bent element, concretely?**
For a 2-node Timoshenko beam, $\gamma(x) = dw/dx - \theta(x)$. Linear shape functions make $dw/dx$ constant but $\theta(x)$ linear, so $\gamma(x)$ is forced away from zero whenever curvature ($\theta_1\neq\theta_2$) is present: $\gamma(x) = (w_2-w_1)/L - \theta_1 - (\theta_2-\theta_1)x/L$. Any real bending drags along spurious shear strain, and since shear stiffness $GA_s \sim Et^2$ while bending stiffness $EI \sim Et^3$, the spurious shear term dominates the energy as thickness $t\to 0$, artificially stiffening the element — the relative damage worsens with slenderness. An equivalent 2D picture: for a Q4 element under pure bending ($u_x=-\kappa xy$, $u_y=\tfrac12\kappa x^2$, exact $\gamma_{xy}=0$), bilinear interpolation with nodal values at $x=\pm a$ gives $\partial u_y^h/\partial x = 0$ while $\partial u_x^h/\partial y = -\kappa x$, so $\gamma_{xy}^h = -\kappa x \neq 0$.

**Q: Is hourglassing a consequence of shear or volumetric locking?**
Neither — it's a side effect of the *cure* (uniform reduced integration), not of locking itself. They're opposite failure modes of the same lever (integration order): too much integration → locking; too little → hourglassing.

**Q: What does "undersampling" mean in a rank/null-space picture?**
$K_e = \sum_{gp} w_{gp}\,B^T(\xi_{gp})\,D\,B(\xi_{gp})\,|J(\xi_{gp})|$. Each Gauss point's contribution has rank $\le$ (number of strain components), so $\operatorname{rank}(K_e) \le n_{gp}\times(\text{strain components})$. By rank–nullity, $\dim(\text{null space}) = (\text{DOFs}) - \operatorname{rank}(K_e)$. For a 4-node quad with 1-point reduced integration: $\operatorname{rank}(K_e)\le 3$, DOFs $=8 \Rightarrow \dim(\text{null space})\ge 5$, but only 3 are legitimate rigid-body modes — the extra $\ge2$ are hourglass modes: displacement patterns whose strain field vanishes exactly at the single sample point (like a sine wave sampled only at its zero crossings) despite storing real energy elsewhere in the element.

**Q: What is Selective Reduced Integration (SRI)?**
Integrate the *problematic* strain term (shear for shear locking, volumetric for volumetric locking) at reduced order, while integrating the well-behaved term (bending/deviatoric) at full order — removing the over-constraint that causes locking without undersampling the terms that trigger hourglassing. A middle path between full integration (locks) and uniform reduced integration (hourglasses).

**Q: What is the B-bar method, in the same rank framework?**
Split strain into deviatoric + volumetric parts. Keep $B_{dev}$ from full shape-function derivatives; replace $B_{vol}$ with a lower-order/averaged $\bar B_{vol}$, still evaluated at the **full** set of Gauss points. Since $n_{gp}$ is unchanged, the ceiling on $\operatorname{rank}(K_e)$ never drops — no hourglass risk introduced. The cure comes from making $\bar B_{vol}$ itself a poorer, lower-rank operator (unable to resolve the incompressible mode finely enough to over-charge it energy), not from sampling fewer points. For linear problems B-bar and SRI can be shown to produce an identical $K_e$ — same cure, different mechanism (under-representing vs. undersampling).

**Q: What are incompatible modes, and how do they relate to hourglassing?**
Enrich the interpolation with extra internal "bubble" DOFs (statically condensed out) that let the element bend with a richer internal curvature field, fixing shear locking without forcing spurious shear strain — the same effect as SRI/B-bar, achieved by adding kinematic freedom rather than weakening the sampling. Quadrature stays fully integrated ($n_{gp}$ untouched), so no hourglass risk is introduced. ("Incompatible" refers to a separate issue: these modes can break inter-element displacement continuity, requiring Taylor's correction term to pass the patch test.)

**Q: What does the patch test establish, and what does it not establish?**
It checks whether an element assembly reproduces a simple exact field — classically, constant strain — under appropriate boundary conditions. Passing demonstrates basic consistency (convergence as $h\to0$ is not ruled out), but it does **not** guarantee good bending behavior, resistance to locking, robustness under severe distortion, accurate contact pressure, or good dynamic performance.

## 4. Strain/Stress Evaluation and Post-Processing

**Q: Where are strains and stresses actually computed — nodes or Gauss points?**
**Gauss (integration) points**, not nodes. The FE solve only produces nodal displacements $\mathbf d$ from $K\mathbf d = F$; strain and stress are derived quantities: $\boldsymbol\varepsilon(\xi) = B(\xi)\,\mathbf d_e$, $\boldsymbol\sigma(\xi) = D\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon(\xi)$. They're evaluated at Gauss points because (a) $B$ is already evaluated there during stiffness assembly, (b) Gauss points are provably **superconvergent** for strain/stress — more accurate than any other location, including the nodes — and (c) nonlinear state variables (plastic strain, contact status) are naturally stored and updated there.

**Q: How do you get nodal stress values for plotting, then?**
Extrapolation + averaging, done as a post-processing step: extrapolate each element's Gauss-point values to its own nodes (using the same or a specially fitted shape function, evaluated outside the Gauss-point range), then average across elements sharing a node. This is why "averaged" vs. "unaveraged" nodal stress is a standard post-processor toggle, and why unaveraged contour plots look discontinuous across element boundaries while averaged ones look smooth.

## 5. Mesh Refinement: h vs. p, and Convergence Rates

**Q: Does halving the element size $h$ halve the $L^2$ error?**
Not quite — that rate belongs to the **energy ($H^1$) norm**, not $L^2$. For polynomial degree $p$: energy-norm error $\sim O(h^p)$; $L^2$-norm error $\sim O(h^{p+1})$ (one order higher, via the Aubin–Nitsche duality trick). For **linear elements** ($p=1$): energy-norm error $\sim O(h)$ (halving $h$ does halve it), but $L^2$ error $\sim O(h^2)$ (halving $h$ quarters it).

**Q: h-refinement vs. p-refinement — how do their convergence behaviors differ?**
- **h-refinement** (fixed $p$): algebraic convergence, $O(h^p)$.
- **p-refinement** (fixed mesh): **exponential** convergence for smooth solutions (spectral-element-like behavior).
- $h$ wins at singularities — no polynomial degree fixes a true singularity locally; $p$ wins where the solution is smooth.
- **hp-refinement**: $h$-refine (graded mesh) at singularities, $p$-refine in smooth regions — recovers exponential convergence even in the presence of singularities.

**Q: Where does h-refinement typically win in practice?**
Fracture mechanics (crack-tip $r^{-1/2}$ singularity), contact mechanics (sharp gradients and near-discontinuities at the contact edge, moving active/inactive set), shock/compaction-front propagation in dynamic problems (steep near-discontinuous front), re-entrant corners/notches, thin boundary/shear layers.

**Q: Where does p-refinement typically win in practice?**
Smooth linear-elastic stress analysis away from singularities, modal/vibration analysis (smooth global mode shapes), heat conduction in smooth homogeneous domains, low-to-moderate-frequency wave propagation/acoustics with smooth fields, smooth-geometry Dirichlet problems with no contact.

**Q: One-line framing of the whole trade-off?**
h-refinement compensates for *lost regularity* (shrink the region a polynomial has to approximate); p-refinement exploits *smoothness that's already there* (more local approximation power per DOF). The same logic drives hp-adaptive methods: spend $h$ where regularity is bad, $p$ where it's good.

## 6. Sources of Nonlinearity and the Tangent Stiffness

**Q: Where does nonlinearity in the FE weak form come from?**
Three independent, combinable sources:
- **Material**: constitutive law $\sigma(\varepsilon)$ nonlinear (plasticity, hyperelasticity) — this alone makes the residual nonlinear even under small-strain kinematics, since the strain–displacement relation stays linear but $\sigma$ vs. $\varepsilon$ does not.
- **Geometric**: the strain measure is nonlinear in displacement (e.g. Green–Lagrange strain's quadratic term); equilibrium and kinematics both depend on the current, unknown configuration.
- **Contact/boundary**: the domain of definition of the residual changes with the solution (evolving gap, active set, normals, friction, topology) — often non-smooth, not just nonlinear, and can make the residual nonlinear even when material and kinematics are both linear.

**Q: What exactly is linearized to build the tangent, and is that before or after discretization?**
The Gateaux/Fréchet derivative of the residual $R$ w.r.t. displacement $u$, in direction $\Delta u$, giving a tangent bilinear form that's then discretized into $K_T = \partial R/\partial \mathbf d$. For standard Galerkin FEM, linearize-then-discretize and discretize-then-linearize commute — "consistent tangent" means doing this consistently either way. In practice (e.g. FEniCS/UFL) the function space is already fixed when differentiating, so it's effectively discretize-then-linearize done symbolically.

**Q: Where do the "dropped higher-order terms" of linearization actually come from?**
$F_{int} = \int \boldsymbol\sigma{:}\delta\boldsymbol\varepsilon\,dV$ involves two Taylor expansions, both truncated:
- **Stress side**: $\sigma(\varepsilon+\Delta\varepsilon) \approx \sigma(\varepsilon) + (\partial\sigma/\partial\varepsilon)\Delta\varepsilon + \text{HOT}$ → the **material tangent**; the truncated terms reflect constitutive nonlinearity, independent of geometry.
- **Strain side**: if strain itself is nonlinear in $u$ (e.g. Green–Lagrange has a $\nabla u^T\nabla u$ term), $\varepsilon(u+\Delta u)$ has a quadratic-in-$\Delta u$ piece → the **geometric tangent**.
- Contact adds a third piece from linearizing the contact traction/gap term, often non-smooth rather than polynomial.

So $K_T = K_{\text{material}} + K_{\text{geometric}}\,(+\,K_{\text{contact}})$: geometric nonlinearity is just the most visible source (explicit quadratic term), not the only one.

**Q: How does linearizing the internal virtual work produce material and geometric stiffness explicitly?**
Starting from $\delta W_{int} = \int_{\Omega_0} \mathbf S{:}\delta\mathbf E\,dV_0$ (reference-configuration virtual work in terms of the second Piola–Kirchhoff stress $\mathbf S$ and Green–Lagrange strain variation $\delta\mathbf E$ — see the Continuum Mechanics FAQ for their definitions), linearization gives

$$\Delta(\delta W_{int}) = \int_{\Omega_0}\Delta\mathbf S:\delta\mathbf E\,dV_0 + \int_{\Omega_0}\mathbf S:\Delta(\delta\mathbf E)\,dV_0$$

Using $\Delta\mathbf S = \mathbf C_{\text{tan}}{:}\Delta\mathbf E$ (the constitutive tangent), the first term gives the **material stiffness** $K_M$; the second — which involves the *existing* stress $\mathbf S$ acting on the second variation of a nonlinear strain measure — gives the **geometric (initial-stress) stiffness** $K_G$. Together, $K_T = K_M + K_G$.
